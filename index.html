02 — People and Nature

How do Australians engage with green spaces?

Australian adult participation rates in selected nature-based activities, by gender, 2011–2012

Participation was relatively balanced between genders across all activities.

The most notable difference was seen in nature conservation advocacy, where the female participation rate was higher by 3.7 percentage points.

Natural environment-related activities attracted the highest participation rate for both genders, at around 73%.

In contrast, for both genders, fewer than 5% participated in paid or voluntary conservation work.

Australian adult participation rates in selected nature-based recreational activities, 2011–2012


National parks and botanic gardens were the most popular nature-based recreational destinations, reported by almost half the respondents in this study, followed by nature walks and camping.

Reasons Australian adults participate in nature-based activities, 2011–2012

Enjoyment was the leading reason for participation, reported by over 90% of participants, followed by social reasons and exercise. Overall, the findings suggest that Australian adults engage with nature primarily for recreation, socialising, and physical activity.

03 — More Than Just Green

Why do these green spaces matter?

The adjusted odds ratio (AOR) in this study compares the odds of experiencing a mental health outcome between children with and without access to public open spaces such as parks.

An AOR below 1 indicates lower odds of the mental health outcome; an AOR above 1 indicates higher odds.

Children with access to public open spaces generally showed better odds of mental health outcomes, particularly when spaces were within 800 metres of home. They were less likely to experience externalising difficulties, and more likely to show higher competence.

Note: Child-friendly space = a public open space with both a playground and a public toilet within 100 metres distance.

Access to public open space and children’s mental health, 2022

How urban green infrastructure supports ecosystem services across four Australian cities (2030 scenarios: BAU and High)

BAU (business as usual) describes the current land use with no change in policy. High increases tree canopy and water retention, and cuts hard surfaces by 35% across the four cities. All values are simulated for 2030.

In the High scenario, every city gains on every service. Across the four cities, the number of days above 35°C per year is estimated to fall from 221 to 129.

Combined, carbon sequestration, carbon retention, air filtration and tree water regulation all approximately double, while recreation space and other water regulation rise by about a third.

04 — An Ever-Changing Landscape

How has Australia’s green cover changed over time?

Percentage change in tree cover across Australian states
and territories, 2016–2020

Tree cover grew in varying amounts across most Australian states and territories, increasing the most in Tasmania (11.52%). Only the Northern Territory saw a slight decline of 0.1%.

Percentage-point change in vegetation cover across land uses in Metropolitan Melbourne, 2014–2018

Looking more closely at Metropolitan Melbourne, transport land recorded the largest increase in vegetation cover (12.5 percentage points). Conversely, residential and primary production land experienced declines (−2.8 and −0.6 percentage points respectively).

Green spaces are about more than how many parks or gardens a city has; accessibility matters just as much as availability.

Access to nature supports wellbeing, while greener urban environments can help reduce extreme heat and strengthen ecosystem services.

Although tree cover is increasing across most Australian states and territories, progress remains uneven, highlighting the need to make green spaces more accessible and protect them for the future.

Notes and Limitations
• Some studies measure public open space rather than green space. Public open space generally includes publicly accessible areas such as parks, gardens and other outdoor recreational spaces. However, not all public open spaces are necessarily green spaces.
